The Reading

The 1950s

Economic Prosperity and Postwar Boom
Following the end of World War II, America was one of the only nations not left devastated by war and experienced a period of economic boom known as the "Postwar Prosperity." This was fueled by various factors including the GI Bill, which provided education and housing benefits to veterans, and the Marshall Plan, which helped to rebuild war-torn Europe and stimulated American exports. Industries such as manufacturing and technology thrived, leading to a surge in employment and a rise in the standard of living for many Americans. Many Americans worked high-paying union jobs and income inequality fell.
Suburbanization and Car Culture
One of the defining features of the 1950s was the phenomenon of suburbanization. As returning veterans and their families sought affordable housing outside of urban centers, suburban communities boomed. In places like Levittown, companies built new neighborhoods of almost identical houses quickly and cheaply. This shift was facilitated by the construction of highways and the expansion of the automobile industry. Car culture became ingrained in American society, symbolizing freedom, mobility, and prosperity. Unfortunately, many of these newly constructed suburbs were racially segregated, and Black Americans were often prevented from moving into these areas. The practice of redlining systematically prevented Black families from building the same wealth through homeownership that white families were accumulating, a gap that has never fully closed. To make matters worse, the growing network of highways being built across America were often routed through predominantly Black neighborhoods, harming their property values or, in some cases, bulldozing the limited housing Black Americans were actually allowed to live in.
The Baby Boom and Teenage Culture
The 1950s saw a significant increase in the birth rate known as the "Baby Boom." These 'boomers' had a profound impact on American society, shaping everything from family dynamics to consumer culture. With the rise of teenagers as a distinct demographic group, pop culture became increasingly influential. Rock and roll music, movies, and television shows reflected the desires and values of this new generation, challenging traditional norms and shaping a unique youth culture. For the first time, teenagers were actually being seen as an important part of society economically and culturally. Musicians like Chuck Berry, Buddy Holly and Elvis Presley spread the new genre of Rock and Roll while actors like James Dean, Marilyn Monroe and Marlon Brando starred in movies that appealed to young audiences.
The Cold War and the Space Race
The 1950s was dominated by the Cold War which led to the Korean War from 1950–53 as well as military buildup, and proxy conflicts around the world. The Space Race emerged as a key battleground in this rivalry, with both superpowers racing to achieve significant milestones in space exploration. In 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. American leaders worried that the Soviet Union was ahead of the US scientifically and technologically, which was especially concerning because the rocket technology used to explore space could also be used to fire nuclear weapons. To correct this, the government pushed for more math and science in the education system. After the Soviets launched the first satellite and the first person into space, the US began to make the space race a huge priority.
The Red Scare
During the 1950s, America was gripped by the Red Scare, a period of intense fear and paranoia regarding communism and communist sympathizers. Led by Senator Joseph McCarthy, the government initiated investigations and 'witch hunts' to root out suspected communists, leading to blacklists, arrests, and the stifling of dissenting voices. People had their careers and reputations destroyed before McCarthyism finally calmed down. The reality was that these fears were massively overblown and this atmosphere of suspicion and conformity cast a shadow over American society and politics, but it did help advance the careers of politicians like Nixon and Reagan who positioned themselves as strongly anti-communist.
The Role of Women and Emergence of Feminism
Despite the emphasis on traditional gender roles in the 1950s, many women entered the workforce during the war and continued to seek greater opportunities and equality in the postwar period. The emergence of a new wave of feminism challenged societal expectations and advocated for women's rights, including access to education, employment, and reproductive freedom. Figures like Betty Friedan and her book The Feminine Mystique sparked conversations about the limitations of traditional gender roles and laid the groundwork for the feminist movement of the 1960s and beyond.
The Eisenhower Years
In 1952, Dwight D. Eisenhower was elected president; he would serve as president for the rest of the '50s. Eisenhower served as a moderate leader and was widely popular, winning both elections easily. Eisenhower focused on maintaining peace during the Cold War, and continued the policy of "containment," which aimed to prevent the spread of communism without direct conflict. Eisenhower preferred using covert (secret) action and coups, using the CIA to overthrow governments if the US feared they were leaning toward communism or even socialism. Overall, his presidency was marked by stability, economic growth, and efforts to manage global tensions. Eisenhower also passed modest civil rights legislation and enforced the Brown v. Board ruling, which were both positive developments, but there were still major racial issues during the 1950s.
The Early Civil Rights Movement
Segregation, racism, and disenfranchisement were major problems during the 1950s, but the early Civil Rights movement gained momentum with landmark events such as the Brown v. Board of Education Supreme Court decision, which declared segregation in public schools unconstitutional, and the Montgomery Bus Boycott, led by Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr. These efforts laid the groundwork for the larger Civil Rights movement of the 1960s, challenging the racial inequalities deeply entrenched in American society, but it wouldn't be until the next decade that major changes started to take place.
Questioning the Consensus
The 1950s are often imagined as an age of consensus and conformity; we often picture this time period as an era when Americans got along, everyone was happy, the middle-class was growing and many people were finally achieving the 'American Dream.' In reality however, it was a decade marked by significant social, political, and cultural upheaval. The Red Scare and McCarthyism fueled paranoia and division, while the Civil Rights movement challenged the racial status quo. Additionally, the emergence of youth culture and the beginnings of feminist activism signaled a growing desire for change and individual expression. The Beatnik Generation of poets and authors created art that criticized conformist attitudes and impacted the counter culture that would emerge even more so in the 1960s. So while the 1950s may have been characterized by a superficial sense of unity and prosperity, beneath the surface there were deep-seated tensions and conflicts that would shape the decades to come.

The Other America
Exclusion , Fear , Conformity
    • The prosperity was explicitly, deliberately built for white families. Black Americans were locked out of the GI Bill's benefits, the suburbs, and the wealth they were creating.
    • The Red Scare and McCarthyism destroyed careers and silenced dissent through fear and accusation rather than evidence.
    • Women were pressured out of the workforce and back into the home — with remarkable success, until the pressure itself became a subject of books, lawsuits, and protests.
    • The nuclear threat was real and constant. Children practiced duck-and-cover drills. Fallout shelters were sold as home appliances.
    • Beneath the surface of consensus, the civil rights movement, feminist awakening, and youth rebellion were already gathering force.

Primary Sources

The Evidence

Levittown, PA (1950s)
A planned suburban development where developers built nearly identical homes at a rate of 30 per day. Levittown became a symbol of postwar prosperity — and racial exclusion.
Ivy Mike H-Bomb Test (1952)
The first successful test of a hydrogen bomb — 500 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb. The nuclear arms race kept Americans in a state of constant, low-level dread.
Duck and Cover Drill
Schoolchildren practiced 'duck and cover' drills throughout the 1950s. What does it tell us about daily life in the nuclear age?
Chuck Berry (1957)
Berry's music blended African American rhythm and blues with a style that crossed racial lines — thrilling teenagers and alarming their parents. Rock and roll was, in many ways, a civil rights story.
Senator Joseph McCarthy
McCarthy's hearings destroyed careers with little evidence. His tactics — accusation as proof — became a model for political demagoguery. What parallels do you see today?
Rosa Parks Arrested (1955)
Parks' arrest for refusing to give up her seat sparked a 381-day boycott that changed America. She later said: 'People always say that I didn't give up my seat because I was tired, but that isn't true.'
