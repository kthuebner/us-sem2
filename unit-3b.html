Photo Analysis, #4

The Vietnam War

Five Photographs That Changed America

Overview

Context

The Vietnam War was the first war fought in front of television cameras, and the photographs that came out of it permanently changed the relationship between the American public, the press, and the military. Images that previous generations never would have seen were now on the front pages of newspapers and on the evening news.

The five photographs on this page are among the most famous ever taken. Each one captured a single moment, but each came to represent something much larger about the Vietnam Era, about protest, about war crimes, about the cost of a conflict that deeply divided the country, and about the relief when it finally ended. As we move through this unit, we will return to these images and discuss what they meant and why they mattered.

Photo 1 The Burning Monk
June 11, 1963
Saigon, South Vietnam
⚠️ Graphic Content This photograph contains disturbing or graphic imagery. It is a historically significant primary source.
Malcolm Browne, Associated Press
What Happened
Thích Quảng Đức, a 73-year-old Buddhist monk, sat down at a busy Saigon intersection, was doused in gasoline by fellow monks, and set himself on fire in an act of protest against the persecution of Buddhists by South Vietnam's Catholic president, Ngô Đình Diệm. He did not flinch or make a sound. Fellow monks and nuns surrounded him in prayer as he burned.
Why It Mattered
The photograph shocked the world and forced international attention onto South Vietnam's political crisis. President Kennedy saw it and said it was the most disturbing image he had ever seen. It accelerated the collapse of the Diệm government, which was overthrown and assassinated months later. The image became a symbol of moral courage and the power of protest. It also raised a fundamental question Americans would keep asking: what kind of government were we fighting to defend?
Who Is Pictured
Thích Quảng Đức, Buddhist monk protesting the persecution of Buddhists by the South Vietnamese government
What It Came to Represent
Shocked the world, forced a political crisis, and contributed to the fall of the Diệm government. Became a symbol of moral protest that reframed American public debate about what the U.S. was defending in Vietnam.
Photo 2 Saigon Execution
February 1, 1968
Saigon, South Vietnam
⚠️ Graphic Content This photograph contains disturbing or graphic imagery. It is a historically significant primary source.
Eddie Adams, Associated Press, Pulitzer Prize 1969
What Happened
During the Tet Offensive, South Vietnamese Brigadier General Nguyễn Ngọc Loan, chief of the National Police, shot Viet Cong prisoner Nguyễn Văn Lém point-blank in the head on a Saigon street. Eddie Adams captured the exact moment the gun fired. Lém had reportedly just executed a South Vietnamese officer and his family.
Why It Mattered
The photograph won the Pulitzer Prize and became one of the most debated images in the history of photojournalism. It horrified the American public, appearing to show a U.S. ally committing a summary execution. It deepened opposition to the war. The man holding the gun, General Loan, was our ally. The man being shot was our enemy. The image made neither side look like heroes. Eddie Adams spent the rest of his life regretting the photo's impact on Loan's reputation, saying the photo was misunderstood without context.
Who Is Pictured
Nguyễn Ngọc Loan (South Vietnamese National Police chief, U.S. ally) executing Nguyễn Văn Lém (Viet Cong prisoner, U.S. enemy)
What It Came to Represent
Pulitzer Prize, 1969. Turned American public opinion against the war. Raised difficult questions about justice, allies, and the rules of war. Eddie Adams later said the photo destroyed a good man's reputation by showing one moment without context.
Photo 3 Kent State
May 4, 1970
Kent State University, Ohio
⚠️ Graphic Content This photograph contains disturbing or graphic imagery. It is a historically significant primary source.
John Filo, student photographer, Pulitzer Prize 1971
What Happened
Following Nixon's announcement that U.S. forces had invaded Cambodia, student protests erupted across America. At Kent State University, the Ohio National Guard opened fire on a crowd of unarmed student protesters, killing four students and wounding nine. Mary Ann Vecchio, a 14-year-old runaway who happened to be on campus, knelt over the body of Jeffrey Miller, 20, and screamed. Student photographer John Filo captured the moment.
Why It Mattered
The photo brought the war home in a way that no battlefield image had. These were American college students, killed by American soldiers, on an American campus. It triggered a nationwide student strike, forced the temporary closure of hundreds of universities, and intensified the antiwar movement. Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution shortly after. The image permanently altered the relationship between the American public, the government, and the Vietnam War.
Who Is Pictured
Mary Ann Vecchio (14-year-old runaway) kneeling over Jeffrey Miller (20-year-old Kent State student, killed by the Ohio National Guard)
What It Came to Represent
Pulitzer Prize, 1971. Triggered a nationwide student strike and the closure of hundreds of campuses. Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution shortly after. Became the defining image of the antiwar movement and the moment many Americans felt the government had gone too far.
Photo 4 My Lai
March 16, 1968
My Lai, South Vietnam
⚠️ Graphic Content This photograph contains disturbing or graphic imagery. It is a historically significant primary source.
Ronald L. Haeberle, U.S. Army photographer
What Happened
U.S. Army soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, under the command of Lt. William Calley, massacred between 347 and 504 unarmed South Vietnamese civilians in the village of My Lai. The victims were mostly women, children, and elderly men. Army photographer Ronald Haeberle documented the aftermath. The massacre was covered up for over a year before journalist Seymour Hersh broke the story in November 1969.
Why It Mattered
The My Lai Massacre became the most damaging revelation of the Vietnam War. It confirmed the worst fears of the antiwar movement and devastated the credibility of U.S. military leadership. Lt. Calley was convicted of murder but served only three years under house arrest. The photos made the abstract horror of war concrete and unignorable. They forced a national reckoning with what American soldiers were doing in Vietnam and remain among the most disturbing documents in American military history.
Who Is Pictured
Civilians massacred by U.S. Army soldiers of Charlie Company under Lt. William Calley. Between 347 and 504 unarmed men, women, and children were killed.
What It Came to Represent
The My Lai Massacre. Covered up for 16 months before journalist Seymour Hersh exposed it. Lt. Calley was convicted but served only house arrest. Became the defining symbol of American war crimes in Vietnam and permanently damaged public trust in the military.
Photo 5 Burst of Joy
March 17, 1973
Travis Air Force Base, California
Sal Veder, Associated Press, Pulitzer Prize 1974
What Happened
Lt. Col. Robert L. Stirm, a U.S. Air Force pilot and prisoner of war held in North Vietnam for over five years, landed at Travis Air Force Base to be reunited with his family. His daughter Lorrie, 15, ran ahead of the rest of the family, arms outstretched, leaping toward her father. AP photographer Sal Veder captured the moment.
Why It Mattered
The photograph won the Pulitzer Prize and became the iconic image of the end of the Vietnam War. After years of horrific images, of burning monks, executions, dead students, and massacred civilians, this single moment of pure joy became the image Americans chose to remember. It symbolized not victory, but relief, the end of a long and painful era. There is a bittersweet coda: Stirm had received a letter on the plane home telling him his wife wanted a divorce. The family in the photo had already begun to fall apart.
Who Is Pictured
Lt. Col. Robert L. Stirm (U.S. Air Force, returned POW) greeted by his children and wife at Travis Air Force Base, California
What It Came to Represent
Pulitzer Prize, 1974. Became the defining image of the end of the Vietnam War. After years of traumatic photography, this moment of joy was the image Americans needed. Poignant footnote: Stirm had just received a letter that his wife was filing for divorce.
