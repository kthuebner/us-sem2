Photo Analysis, #1

Japanese Internment Camps

Dorothea Lange & Ansel Adams

Background

Context

After Japan bombed Pearl Harbor in December 1941, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, authorizing the forced removal of approximately 110,000 Japanese and Japanese-Americans from their homes on the West Coast. They were sent to remote War Relocation Authority camps for the duration of the war, without trial, charges, or due process.

Two acclaimed American photographers documented the camps, but from very different vantage points, with very different purposes, and with very different outcomes for their work.

The Photographers

Biographies
Lange

Dorothea Lange

1895, 1965
Migrant Mother, 1936

Dorothea Lange is best known for her documentary work during the Great Depression. While working for the Farm Security Administration, she photographed migrant workers, displaced families, and the human cost of the Dust Bowl. Her image Migrant Mother (1936) became one of the most iconic photographs in American history.

Lange believed photography was a tool for social change, a way to make injustice visible to those who might otherwise ignore it. When the U.S. government hired her to photograph the internment process, it hoped she would show the relocation as orderly and humane. She did not.

Her hundreds of photographs captured confusion, fear, and the stripping away of ordinary American life. The government seized her photos before they could be published. They were suppressed until after the war ended.

Approach: Documentary realism. Show hardship honestly. Use photography as witness and protest.
Adams

Ansel Adams

1902, 1984
Tetons & Snake River, 1942

Ansel Adams is the most celebrated landscape photographer in American history. Famous for his sweeping, technically precise black-and-white images of the American West, his work helped define how Americans visualize their wilderness.

Adams visited Manzanar in 1943 at the personal invitation of camp director Ralph Merritt, a close friend. He was not hired by the government. He came on his own terms, with his own agenda.

His goal was to challenge internment by showing its victims as dignified, resilient, patriotic Americans. He was barred from photographing guard towers or barbed wire, so he focused instead on faces, baseball games, chapels, and community life.

Unlike Lange, Adams was allowed to publish his photos, before the war ended, in a 1944 book titled Born Free and Equal, warning about the dangers of letting wartime hysteria justify stripping U.S. citizens of their freedom.

Approach: Dignified portraiture. Show humanity and resilience. Challenge internment by humanizing its victims.
Photo Analysis, What to Look For

As you examine the photographs below, consider the following questions:

  1. Who or what is the subject? What does the photographer choose to show, and what do they leave out?
  2. What is the mood or tone? How do lighting, composition, and framing shape how you feel about what you are seeing?
  3. What was the photographer's purpose? Was this photograph meant to document, protest, humanize, or persuade?
  4. How does knowing the photographer's background change how you read the image?
  5. How did each photographer try to point out injustice? Which approach do you think was more effective?

Discussion

Which approach do you think was the most impactful?

Dorothea Lange
Hired by the government to document the relocation process, Lange refused to make it look acceptable. Her photos showed chaos, fear, and injustice. She captured shuttered businesses, stressed faces, crowded buses, and threadbare barracks. The government recognized what she had done and seized her photographs, suppressing them for the remainder of the war. Her work could not speak to anyone until the damage was already done.
Ansel Adams
Adams went to Manzanar on his own terms, not hired by the government. Restricted from photographing barbed wire or guard towers, he focused on baseball games, chapels, community meetings, and faces, portraying internees as loyal and dignified Americans. The government allowed him to publish his photos precisely because they served a different purpose. His book Born Free and Equal appeared in 1944, while the war was still ongoing and while people were still imprisoned.
